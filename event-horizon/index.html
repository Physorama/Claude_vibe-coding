What you're seeing

The shadow. Any ray aimed inside an impact parameter of b = 3√3⁄2 rs ≈ 2.60 rs falls in. The dark disk is that capture cross-section, about 2.6× wider than the horizon itself.

The photon ring. At r = 1.5 rs light can orbit. Rays grazing this sphere loop around one or more times before escaping, stacking thin copies of the whole sky and disk into a razor-bright ring.

The halo over the top. The arch above and below the shadow is the far side of the disk. Its light climbs over the hole and is bent toward you. You are seeing the back and the underside of the disk at once.

Doppler beaming. Inner gas orbits at about half the speed of light. The side moving toward you is blueshifted and brightened by roughly g⁴. The receding side dims and reddens. Turn the slider to 0 to get the symmetric, softer look from the film Interstellar.

Gravitational redshift. Light climbing out from radius r loses energy by a factor of √(1 − rs/r). That is why the inner edge cools even though the gas there is hottest.

The inner edge. Stable circular orbits stop at the ISCO, r = 3 rs. Inside it, gas plunges and the disk ends.

Method. Each pixel integrates a = −1.5 h² r⃗ ⁄ r⁵, which reproduces the null geodesics of the Schwarzschild metric exactly in shape. A leapfrog step scaled with r is used, and the disk plane is tested for crossings on every step.
